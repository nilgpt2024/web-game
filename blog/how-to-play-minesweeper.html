How to Play Minesweeper: Rules and Logic to Win

Minesweeper looks like a game of luck — a wall of gray squares with no hint of where to start. In reality it is a deduction game in disguise: every number on the board tells you exactly what it hides, and a methodical player can clear a full board without guessing once. This guide explains the rules, how to read the numbers, and the simple routine that turns random clicking into logic.

What Minesweeper is#

Minesweeper did not begin with Windows. Grid-based mine-clearing games existed on mainframe computers years earlier, where a player probed a minefield one square at a time. The version the world remembers arrived in 1990, when Microsoft bundled Minesweeper into the Windows Entertainment Pack and later shipped it with Windows itself. Millions of office workers discovered the game on a slow afternoon, and it famously taught a generation the purpose of the right mouse button — you use it to plant a flag on a square you have identified as a mine. Today it remains one of the most played logic games ever, and the browser version on AIGameHub keeps the rules identical.

The board and the rules#

The rules take a minute to learn:

  • The grid is a minefield: a hidden number of squares contain mines, and the rest are safe.
  • Left-click a square to reveal it. If it contains a mine, the game ends.
  • If it is safe, it shows a number — or a blank, if no mines touch it.
  • A number tells you exactly how many mines sit in the up to eight squares surrounding it.
  • Right-click a square to place a flag, marking a square you believe is mined. Flagged squares cannot be clicked by accident.
  • You win when every safe square is revealed. You do not need to flag every mine — flags are a tool, not the goal.
One subtletyThe first click is safe. Modern versions guarantee that the square you open first (and often its neighborhood) contains no mine, which is why you should always start by opening a few squares in the center rather than a corner.

Reading the numbers#

A number is a tiny census of its neighborhood. A 3 means exactly three mines touch that square — no more, no less. The shape of the board matters a great deal here: a square in the middle of the field has eight neighbors, an edge square has five, and a corner square has only three. A 3 on an edge is a much denser statement than a 3 in open ground, because there are fewer squares left to hide the mines in. Keep this in mind and the numbers become easier to satisfy: an edge 1 with a single hidden neighbor leaves you nothing to decide, while an edge 3 with three hidden neighbors tells you all of them are mines.

A beginner’s step routine#

Here is the routine that clears most beginner boards without a single guess:

  1. Open a central pocket. Start in the middle of the grid and reveal a patch of blank cells. Blank squares reveal automatically in chains, and the numbers around the edge of the pocket are your first clues.
  2. Find a number with exactly one hidden neighbor. If a 1 has one unrevealed neighbor, that neighbor must be a mine. Flag it.
  3. Apply the fully-flagged rule. If a number already has as many flags around it as its value — a 2 with two flags, say — then every other hidden neighbor is safe. Click them.
  4. Repeat the pair. Each newly revealed number creates new neighbors to inspect. The pocket grows outward until it meets the edge of the board.
  5. Use chording. When you are sure a number’s mines are all flagged, middle-click (or click with both buttons) on the number to reveal all its remaining neighbors at once. Chording is the difference between a slow game and a fast one.
The golden ruleA number is solved the moment its flags match its value, and not before. If you flag a 2 with one flag and then click a neighbor, you are gambling — finish the deduction first.

Common edge patterns#

Three patterns repeat constantly along edges and walls. Learn them and you will read whole rows at a glance:

PatternWhat it means
1-1Two adjacent ones along an edge: the square beyond the second 1 is safe.
1-2A one beside a two: the square beyond the 2 is a mine, and the square beyond the 1 is safe.
1-2-1Three in a row: mines sit beyond the two ones, and the square beyond the 2 is safe.

Each pattern is just the two core rules applied twice — a fully accounted number makes its other neighbors safe, and a number with one remaining neighbor forces a mine. Once you see them as shorthand rather than magic, they become easy to re-derive on any board.

When guessing is unavoidable#

Every pattern above is deduction, but not every board is fully deducible. A small fraction of positions — most famously the 50/50 endgame — offer two squares with identical evidence and no way to tell which one is mined. This is not a failure of technique; it is built into the game. When you reach a 50/50, the only honest move is to pick one, click, and move on. Experienced players plan around this by opening large safe pockets early and solving everything they can, but they accept that occasionally a board ends in a coin flip. Knowing when the logic has genuinely run out is part of the skill — clicking wildly before that point is the real mistake.

Beginner mistakes#

  • Random clicking to start. Reveal a central pocket deliberately. Every click after the first few should be based on a number you can read.
  • Over-flagging. Flagging squares you merely suspect wastes time and buries the numbers you need. Flag only what the numbers force.
  • Miscounting neighbors. It is easy to forget diagonals. A number counts all eight surrounding squares, including corners — double-check before you clear a cell.
  • Chording before the flags are complete. Chording on an unsolved number treats your guesses as facts and can reveal a mine.

Practice and next steps#

Play the Minesweeper game on AIGameHub and start on the smallest grid. The routine above clears beginner boards almost entirely by logic, and the numbers section becomes instinct after a few rounds. When that feels mechanical, raise the difficulty and practice the edge patterns until they are instant.
