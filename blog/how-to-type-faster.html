How to Type Faster: Practical Tips and Daily Practice

Typing speed is a habit, not a talent. Hunt-and-peck typists who have typed for decades rarely pass forty words per minute, while a focused beginner using the right technique can reach fifty or sixty within a few months. The difference is not practice alone — it is practicing the correct movements. This guide covers how speed is measured, how to set up your hands and desk, and a fifteen-minute daily routine you can actually keep.

How typing speed is measured#

Speed is measured in words per minute (WPM): the number of words you can type in one minute. A “word” is standardized as five characters — letters, spaces, and punctuation all count — so a 300-character minute is scored as 60 WPM regardless of the actual words used. That convention exists because average English words are about five letters long, and it lets different typing tests compare fairly against each other.

Accuracy matters just as much as raw speed. Typing tests usually report both, and many report a net score that subtracts errors: type 80 WPM with ten percent errors and your effective speed is far lower than a clean 40 WPM, because every correction costs real seconds. On a real keyboard the same logic applies — a fast but sloppy typist spends the savings on backspacing.

Setting up your desk#

Good speed starts with a body that is not fighting the desk. Sit with your back straight, shoulders relaxed, and elbows at roughly ninety degrees so your forearms slope slightly down to the keyboard. The keyboard should sit at a height where your wrists stay straight, not bent up or down; a wrist rest helps only if it supports your palms while your fingers hover over the keys. The top of your screen should sit around eye level, about an arm’s length away, so you are not craning forward. And keep your hands light — many typists slow themselves down by pressing keys harder than needed.

The home row and finger mapping#

Touch typing works because every key has exactly one owner. Rest your fingers on the home row: the left hand on A S D F and the right hand on J K L ;. The F and J keys carry small raised bumps, which exist so you can find home position without looking. From that anchor, each finger reaches its own column of keys:

FingerKeys it owns
Left pinkyQ, A, Z, and the left modifiers (Shift, Caps Lock, Ctrl)
Left ringW, S, X
Left middleE, D, C
Left indexR, T, F, G, V, B
Right indexY, U, H, J, N, M
Right middleI, K, and the comma
Right ringO, L, and the period
Right pinkyP, the semicolon, the slash, and the right-edge keys (Enter, Shift, Backspace)

On the number row the same fingers extend upward: the left index handles 4 and 5, the right index handles 6 and 7, and so on outward. The point is never to memorize a diagram but to let every finger return to its home key after each reach — the bumps on F and J are your compass, and the numbers become second nature surprisingly fast.

The golden ruleReturn to home after every keystroke. Speed comes from the return, not the reach — a finger that drifts off its home key forces your eyes down to find it again.

Look at the screen, not the keys#

The single biggest change is also the hardest: stop looking at your hands. If you must, cover the keyboard with a cloth or a blank sheet of paper for ten minutes a day — your fingers will hunt, slow down, and then adapt. Looking down trains your eyes to be the error-corrector, which keeps your fingers lazy. Looking at the screen trains your fingers to know the layout for themselves. Expect the first sessions to feel clumsy; that is the cost of moving responsibility from your eyes to your hands, and it passes within a week or two.

Accuracy before speed#

Trying to type faster by typing faster rarely works. If you push your hands beyond what your fingers know, errors spike, and every error interrupts the rhythm that speed is built on. Instead, set a ceiling on errors: if a drill session produces more than about one mistake per ten words, slow down until it is clean. Speed follows accuracy naturally — as correct keystrokes become automatic, your fingers simply move faster. The typists who plateau are usually the ones who never cleaned up their errors first.

A 15-minute daily plan#

Consistency beats marathon sessions. Fifteen focused minutes a day, six days a week, will move your WPM faster than a three-hour binge once a month. A workable plan looks like this:

  1. Warm up (2 minutes). Type a few familiar sentences or a home-row drill — asdf jkl; — to wake up the finger-to-key mapping.
  2. Focused drill (5 minutes). Target one weakness: a row you avoid, a finger that lags, or a common pair like “th” and “he” that appears constantly in English.
  3. Timed test (3 minutes). Run a one-minute test and write down the WPM and accuracy. The number is feedback, not judgment.
  4. Game time (5 minutes). Play a typing game to keep the session fun — games force speed while your attention is on the score, not on your hands.

Track the daily number somewhere simple. A slow, steady upward line is the most motivating chart you can make, and it tells you honestly whether a change in technique is working.

Common mistakes#

  • Hunt and peck with speed. Pointing with two fingers feels fast enough to fool you, but it caps most people around 30-40 WPM and strains your eyes.
  • Slouching and hovering wrists. Bad posture creates fatigue, and fatigue destroys accuracy late in a session.
  • Watching the keyboard. Your eyes cannot help your fingers learn while they keep doing the work.
  • Racing before accuracy. An error rate above a few percent means you are practicing sloppiness, and practice makes it permanent.
  • Skipping days. A missed week undoes more progress than a bad session.

Frequently asked questions#

I have hit a plateau. What now?

Plateaus are normal around the speed you have held for a few weeks. Raise the difficulty instead of the pace: type less common words, add punctuation, or practice numbers. New material forces your fingers to renegotiate, and speed resumes.

How long until I get noticeably faster?

Most consistent learners add 5-10 WPM in the first month and reach 40-60 WPM within three to six months of daily practice. “Fast” for everyday typing is around 60 WPM; professional typists exceed 100.

Can I really break hunt and peck as an adult?

Yes. Muscle memory is built by repetition, not age. The technique transfers in a few weeks of deliberate practice — the reason most people never break the habit is that they never practice deliberately.
