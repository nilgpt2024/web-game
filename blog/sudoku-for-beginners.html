Sudoku for Beginners: Rules and Step-by-Step Strategy

Sudoku looks like arithmetic, but it is nothing of the sort. There is no adding, subtracting, or guessing required — only logic and patience. A well-made puzzle can be solved with pure deduction: every number on the grid is forced, and the satisfying part is finding out why. This guide walks through the rules, a repeatable step-by-step strategy, and the mistakes beginners make, so you can move from your first easy puzzle to confident solving.

What Sudoku is#

The puzzle we now call Sudoku was not invented in Japan, although that is where it got its name. It was designed in the United States by Howard Garns, a retired architect, who published it in 1979 under the name “Number Place” in a puzzle magazine. The game crossed the Pacific, where a Japanese publisher called Nikoli picked it up, refined the layout, and gave it the name Sudoku — roughly “the digits must remain single” — because every digit may appear only once in its row, column, and box. For decades it stayed a niche Japanese pastime. Then, in 2004, a major British newspaper began printing it daily, and the global boom began. Today it is one of the most widely played logic puzzles in the world, and browser versions like the one on AIGameHub have made it free and instant.

The board and the rules#

You play on a 9×9 grid that is divided into nine smaller 3×3 boxes, each drawn with heavier lines so the structure is easy to see. Some cells are already filled with numbers when the puzzle starts; these are called givens, and they never change. Your job is to fill every empty cell so that three conditions hold at the same time:

  • Each row contains the digits 1 through 9 exactly once.
  • Each column contains the digits 1 through 9 exactly once.
  • Each 3×3 box contains the digits 1 through 9 exactly once.

That is the entire rule set. A well-constructed Sudoku has exactly one solution: a single arrangement that satisfies all three conditions, and every number you place correctly is part of that same solution. Difficulty is not simply a matter of how many givens the puzzle has — though fewer givens usually means harder — but of how easily those givens force the rest of the grid. An easy puzzle resolves with simple steps; a hard one hides its logic several layers deep.

One subtletyA digit is only solved when it fits its row, column, and box at the same time. A number that works for its row but clashes inside its box is wrong, and it will poison everything you build on top of it.

A step-by-step strategy#

Beginners often start by hunting for a number that “looks like it fits” and writing it down. That is filling, not solving. The reliable method is systematic, and it has six steps:

  1. Scan for forced cells. Look at each row, column, and box and ask whether any empty cell can legally hold only one digit. If the other eight digits are already accounted for in that row, column, and box, the missing digit is forced. Place it and move on.
  2. Crosshatch one digit at a time. Pick a digit, say 7, and work it across the whole grid. For each box, trace the rows and columns where a 7 already appears; if a box has only one empty cell that is not covered by an existing 7, that cell must be 7. Crosshatching is the single most powerful beginner tool.
  3. Write pencil marks. When you cannot place any more digits directly, enter small candidate notes in every empty cell — the digits still possible there. Keep them tiny and neat; you will update them as the puzzle resolves.
  4. Find naked and hidden singles. A naked single is a cell with exactly one candidate left; place it immediately. A hidden single is subtler: a candidate that fits in only one cell of a row, column, or box, even though other candidates share that cell. Hidden singles are the workhorses of easy and medium puzzles.
  5. Eliminate with pairs. When two cells in the same unit share the same two candidates, those digits are locked to those two cells — erase them from every other cell in that unit. Pointing pairs work across boxes: if a digit appears in only one row (or column) inside a box, the rest of that row (or column) outside the box cannot contain it.
  6. Re-scan instead of guessing. After every placement the board changes: a placed digit narrows candidates everywhere it appears. The fastest solvers re-run the scan, not their intuition. When you are stuck, hunt for a new forced cell before you ever consider a guess.
The golden ruleNever guess. Every well-made puzzle yields to logic alone. If you feel the urge to guess, you have probably missed a hidden single — step back and re-scan the row, column, and box around the tightest area of the board.

Sudoku vocabulary#

Five terms cover most of what you will read in solving guides and strategy articles:

TermMeaning
GivensThe numbers printed in the grid at the start, which never change.
CandidatesThe possible digits for an empty cell, often noted as small pencil marks.
BoxOne of the nine 3×3 subgrids; also called a block or region.
Naked singleA cell with only one candidate left, so its digit is forced.
Hidden singleA digit that fits in only one cell of a row, column, or box, even though other candidates share that cell.

Beginner mistakes#

  • Guessing and backtracking. One wrong digit can quietly break a puzzle that was otherwise sound. Trust the logic; if you guessed, undo it and find the step you skipped.
  • Skipping lines. It is easy to check rows and columns but forget that every placement must also fit its 3×3 box. Always check the box.
  • Starting too hard. A brutal puzzle on day one teaches frustration, not technique. Build skill on easy and medium grids before you attempt the hard ones.
  • Messy pencil marks. Candidates written in illegible clutter cause misreads and false placements. Keep notes small, even, and organized.

Frequently asked questions#

Is Sudoku a math game?

No. The digits are just symbols; you never add, subtract, or multiply. The game is pure logic — placement and elimination — which is exactly why it appeals to people who insist they hate math.

What makes a puzzle easy or hard?

Easy puzzles mostly resolve with naked singles and simple crosshatching. Hard puzzles add deeper layers: hidden singles tucked further away, locked pairs, and advanced techniques such as X-Wings and swordfish. The rules never change — only the depth of reasoning required.

Can a Sudoku have more than one solution?

Only if it is badly constructed. A proper Sudoku has exactly one solution, and a puzzle that offers two is considered defective rather than a bonus.
