Rock Paper Scissors — Game Introduction

Rock Paper Scissors is the classic three-way contest with no hidden information and no possible advantage except prediction. Rock beats scissors, scissors beats paper, paper beats rock: every choice is beaten by exactly one other, which is what makes the game a clean model of cyclic advantage.

This browser version pits you against the computer in a running match, with both scores displayed. Played genuinely randomly the game is a coin flip, so the interest lies in whether you can read your own habits — most people fall into patterns without noticing, and a simple opponent can exploit them.

How to Play

Choose rock, paper or scissors each round. The computer picks at the same moment, the result is resolved, and the winning side's score increases. Draws add nothing. The match continues until you reset it, and both scores stay visible.

Because the rules are symmetric, there is no strategy that beats a truly random opponent. Against a predictable one, the only edge is pattern-breaking: change your own choice sequence so that it cannot be mapped.

Controls

  • Three choice buttons — rock, paper or scissors
  • Score panel — your score and the computer's, updated each round
  • Reset — clears the match and starts again

Strategy & Tips

  1. Do not repeat a winning choice. The habit of using a move again because it worked is the most exploitable pattern in the game.
  2. Aim for genuine randomness. If your own sequence can be predicted, so can it be countered.
  3. Avoid countering your last loss. Losing players systematically switch to the move that beats what beat them, which is itself a pattern.
  4. Treat draws as neutral. Tracking them adds noise without information; only wins and losses carry signal.
  5. Use the score to calibrate, not to explain. A short run of losses against a random opponent is normal variance.

Background

The game appears across Asia under many names — jan-ken in Japan, and hand-game variants in China and Korea — and reached the West through nineteenth-century Japan. Its modern fame comes from game theory, where the three-way cycle is the standard teaching example of a non-transitive relation, and from international tournament play.

FAQ

What are the rules of Rock Paper Scissors?

Rock beats scissors, scissors beats paper and paper beats rock. Identical choices are a draw.

Can I win consistently against the computer?

Only if its choices are predictable. Against genuinely random play, no strategy produces a long-term edge.

How is the match scored?

Each round adds one point to the winner's side. Draws score nothing, and both totals remain visible until you reset.

Is there a best first move?

No. All three choices are symmetric — each beats one and loses to one — so the first move carries no advantage.
