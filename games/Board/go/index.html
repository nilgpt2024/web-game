Rules

  • Black plays first. Place stones on intersections of the grid lines.
  • Stones of the same color connected horizontally/vertically form a group.
  • A group's liberties are the number of empty adjacent intersections.
  • When a group has zero liberties, it is captured and removed from the board.
  • You may not play a stone that would have no liberties (suicide), unless it captures opponent stones.
  • Ko rule: you may not recreate the immediately previous board position.
  • Either player may pass. Two consecutive passes end the game.
  • Scoring (Chinese/area rules): stones on the board + surrounded empty territory.

About Go

Go is a two-player abstract strategy game played on a 19×19 grid (with smaller 9×9 and 13×13 variants for shorter games). Each player places stones to surround territory and capture enemy groups. Despite simple rules, Go has more legal positions than atoms in the observable universe — it's widely considered the deepest abstract strategy game ever invented.

How to play

Black plays first, then players alternate placing one stone of their color on any empty intersection. A stone or group with no adjacent empty points (no liberties) is captured. This version uses area scoring: your stones on the board plus the empty points they surround. Captured stones are not added separately. Komi (a points adjustment) compensates White for going second.

Frequently asked questions

How is Go different from chess?

Both are abstract strategy with perfect information, but Go favors positional accumulation over tactical exchange. The game tree is far larger; computer mastery only arrived in 2016 with AlphaGo.

What is the ko rule?

You may not immediately recapture a single stone in a way that recreates the previous board position. Prevents infinite capture loops. Larger ko fights are central to high-level strategy.

What's a good board size for beginners?

9×9 — completes in 10–15 minutes and reveals territory and capture concepts cleanly. Move to 13×13 once captures feel natural, 19×19 for full-board strategy.

What is komi?

A points compensation given to White (the second player) at the end. Modern komi is 6.5 or 7.5, fractional to prevent draws.

How this version works

Rules as implemented

  • Board sizes are 9×9, 13×13 and 19×19.
  • You play Black and move first. There are no handicap stones.
  • Scoring is Chinese-style area scoring: each side's stones on the board plus the empty points that touch only that side's stones. Empty areas that touch both colours count for nobody. Captured stones are shown but do not add to the score.
  • Komi is 6.5 points for White on every board size.
  • Suicide is illegal. A move that captures first is checked after the captured stones are removed, so a capturing move that would otherwise have no liberties is allowed.
  • Ko is the simple rule: you cannot play a move that recreates the board exactly as it was one move ago. A pass clears this restriction.

Ending the game and marking dead stones

Two passes in a row open a marking step. Click any group to mark it dead; the whole group toggles together and the score updates straight away, with dead stones treated as empty points for the side that surrounds them. Confirm locks in the final score. Resume Play restores the position exactly as it was before the passes, gives the move to the correct player and needs two new passes before scoring again.

How the computer plays White

The computer looks one move ahead. It tries every legal point, gives each a score and then picks at random among the moves within 2 points of the best, so it does not repeat the same game. The score rewards capturing (20 per stone), rescuing its own group from atari, and putting your groups in atari. It penalises moves that leave its own group in atari, and strongly penalises filling one of its own eyes. Smaller bonuses go to touching its own stones, having more liberties, playing nearer the centre, star points and the third and fourth lines, with a penalty for the first and second lines. Early in the game it also avoids playing far away from every existing stone.

It waits about 0.6 seconds before each move. It passes only when it has no legal move left.

What this version does not have

There is no undo, no saved games and no difficulty setting; reloading the page starts a new game.

Go Rules for Beginners (Baduk / Weiqi)

Go — known as Baduk in Korea and Weiqi in China — is the ancient game of territory and influence. Two players place black and white stones on the intersections of a grid, competing to surround more of the board. The AIGameHub build is single-player versus an AI and offers three board sizes: 9×9 for a quick game, 13×13 for a middle ground, and the full 19×19. The rules are short; the depth is famous.

Placing stones

Black plays first. Stones go on the intersections of the grid lines, not the squares, and once placed a stone never moves — it can only be captured. Same-color stones connected horizontally or vertically (never diagonally) form a single group that lives or dies together.

Liberties and capture

A group's liberties are the empty intersections directly adjacent to it. As long as a group has at least one liberty it stays on the board. Fill its last liberty and the whole group is captured and removed. You may not play a stone that would leave your own group with zero liberties (a suicide) — unless that same move captures an enemy group and thereby gains a liberty.

The ko rule: you may not play a move that recreates the exact board position of the previous turn. This stops endless back-and-forth recapture of a single stone; you must play elsewhere first, then you can return.

Ending the game: pass, pass, then mark the dead

When neither player wants to add stones, they pass. Two consecutive passes end the game. The board then enters a scoring step: click any group of stones that cannot avoid capture to mark it as dead — those stones are removed and count for the surrounding player. Press Confirm / Final Score when both sides agree, or Resume Play if there is a disagreement to settle on the board.

Territory vs area scoring

There are two scoring traditions, and it helps to know both:

  • Territory scoring (Japanese) — count empty intersections you surround, plus prisoners you captured.
  • Area scoring (Chinese) — count empty intersections you surround plus your own live stones on the board.

They usually give the same winner, differing by at most a point or two. This build uses area (Chinese) scoring: your score is your stones on the board plus the empty territory you enclose.

Beginner strategy

  1. Start in the corners, then the sides — corners are easiest to enclose because the board edges do part of the surrounding for you.
  2. Keep your groups connected and give them room to make two separate eyes (enclosed empty points), which makes them uncapturable.
  3. Do not chase every stone. Trading a small capture for a large framework of influence is usually the winning choice.
  4. Learn on 9×9. A small board teaches life, death, and capture in minutes before you scale up to 19×19.
